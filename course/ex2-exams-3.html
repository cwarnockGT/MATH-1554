    Problem 2.2.1.

    (8 points) Suppose \(A\) is an \(m \times n\) matrix and \(\vec b\in \mathbb R ^{m}\) unless otherwise stated. Select true if the statement is true for all choices of \(A\) and \(\vec b\text{.}\) Otherwise, select false.
    🔗

    (a)

    True   /   False:   If \(A\) and \(B\) are row equivalent, then \(\det (A) = \det (B)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Do row operations change the determinant?
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    False. Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Then \(A\) and \(B\) are row equivalent, but \(\det(A) = 1\) and \(\det(B) = 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    True   /   False:   If \(\vec{p}\) and \(\vec{q} \) are probability vectors in \(\mathbb R ^3\text{,}\) then \(\frac{1}{3} \vec{p} + \frac{2}{3} \vec{q}\) is also a probability vector in \(\mathbb R ^3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Recall that a probability vector in \(\mathbb R ^3\) is a vector with nonnegative entries that sum to 1.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    True. Since \(\vec{p}\) and \(\vec{q} \) are probability vectors, their entries are nonnegative and sum to 1. The linear combination \(\frac{1}{3} \vec{p} + \frac{2}{3} \vec{q}\) will also have nonnegative entries and the sum of its entries will be \(\frac{1}{3} + \frac{2}{3} = 1\text{,}\) making it a probability vector.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    True   /   False:   If the matrix product \(AB \) is defined, then the product \(BA\) is also defined.
    🔗
    Hint.
    For a matrix product to be defined, the number of columns of the first matrix must equal the number of rows of the second matrix.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    False. Consider \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \) and \(B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \text{.}\) Then \(AB = [1] \) is defined, but \(BA \) is not defined (The number of columns of \(A \) does not equal the number of rows of \(B \)).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    True   /   False:   If \(\det (A) = 0\text{,}\) then \(A\) must have a zero row or have two rows which are identical.
    🔗
    Hint.
    \(\det (A) = 0 \) when \(A \) is not invertible.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    False. Consider \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp 6 \end{bmatrix} \text{.}\) Then \(\det(A) = 0 \text{,}\) but \(A \) does not have a zero row and does not have two identical rows.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    True   /   False:   If \(A\) is an \(n \times n\) matrix and \(A = LU\) is an \(LU\) factorization of \(A\text{,}\) then \(\det(A) = \det(U)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Use the multiplicity of the determinant.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    True. Since \(A = LU \text{,}\) we have \(\det(A) = \det(LU) \text{.}\) By the multiplicity of the determinant, this becomes \(\det(A) = \det(L) \det(U) \text{.}\) The matrix \(L \) is lower triangular with ones on the diagonal, so \(\det(L) = 1 \text{.}\) Therefore, \(\det(A) = \det(U) \text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    True   /   False:   If \(A\) and \(B\) are invertible \(n \times n\) matrices then \(A + B\) is also invertible.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Play around with some \(2 \times 2 \) examples.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    False. Consider \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \) and \(B = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix} \text{.}\) Then \(A \) and \(B \) are both invertible, but \(A + B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \) is not invertible.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (g)

    True   /   False:   If \(A \) is an \(n \times n \) matrix and \(c\) is a scalar such that \(\det(A - cI_n) = 0\text{,}\) then there exists a nonzero vector \(\vec{v} \in \mathbb R ^n\) such that \(A \vec{v} \in \text{span}\{\vec{v} \}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Relate everything to eigenvectors.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    True. The condition \(\det(A - cI_n) = 0 \) means that \(c \) is an eigenvalue of \(A \text{.}\) By the definition of eigenvectors, there exists a nonzero vector \(\vec{v} \in \mathbb R^n \) such that \(A \vec{v} = c \vec{v} \text{.}\) Therefore, \(A \vec{v} \in \text{span}\{\vec{v} \} \text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (h)

    True   /   False:   If \(A\) and \(B\) are both \(n \times n\) matrices with pivots in each of their columns, then the product \(AB\) has a pivot in each of its columns
    🔗
    Hint.
    Use the Invertible Matrix Theorem
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    True. By the Invertible Matrix Theorem, if \(A \) and \(B \) have pivots in each of their columns, then both \(A \) and \(B \) are invertible (\(A,B \) are squre, so IMT applies). The product of two invertible matrices is also invertible, and therefore \(AB \) has a pivot in each of its columns.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Problem 2.2.2.

    (4 points) Indicate whether the following situations are possible or impossible.
    🔗

    (a)

    Possible   /   Impossible:   A \(2 \times 2\) matrix \(A\) with \(\text{dim}(\Nul(A)) = 0\) such that the system \(A \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}\) has no solution.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Use the Rank-Nullity Theorem
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Impossible. By the Rank-Nullity Theorem, if \(\text{dim}(\Nul(A)) = 0 \text{,}\) then \(\rank(A) = 2 \text{.}\) Since \(A \) is \(2 \times 2 \text{,}\) \(A \) is invertible, and thus \(A \) is both onto and one-to-one. Equivalently, system \(A \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \) must have a solution, and such a solution must be unique.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Possible   /   Impossible:   A \(3 \times 5\) matrix \(A\) so that \(\rank(A) = 4\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Play around with some examples of \(3 \times 5 \) matrices and their ranks.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Impossible. The maximum rank of a \(3 \times 5 \) matrix is \(3 \) (the minimum of the number of rows and columns), so it cannot have rank \(4 \text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Possible   /   Impossible:   Two \(2 \times 2\) non-zero matrices A and B, neither of which is \(I_2\text{,}\) with \(AB = BA\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Try some diagonal matrices.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Possible. For example, let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix} \) and \(B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} \text{.}\) Then \(AB = BA \text{,}\) and neither \(A \) nor \(B \) is \(I_2 \text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Possible   /   Impossible:   A \(2 \times 2\) invertible matrix \(A = \begin{bmatrix}a \amp b \\ c \amp d\end{bmatrix}\) so that \(\det (A) = \det\begin{pmatrix}a \amp b \\ 2a + c \amp 2b + d\end{pmatrix}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Consider the effect of row operations on the determinant.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Possible. For example, let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \text{.}\) Then \(\det(A) = 1 \) and \(\det\begin{pmatrix}1 \amp 0 \\ 2 \amp 1\end{pmatrix} = 1 \text{,}\) so the condition is satisfied. In fact, any invertible matrix will work, since row replacement operations do not change the determinant.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Problem 2.2.3.

    (2 points) Let \(A\) be an \(n \times n\) matrix such that for every \(\vec{b} \in \mathbb R ^n\text{,}\) the system \(A \vec{x} = \vec{b}\) has a unique solution. Which of the following statements is FALSE? \(\textit{Select only one}\)
    🔗
    1. The rows of \(A\) form a basis for \(\mathbb R ^n\)
      🔗
    2. dim \(\{ \vec{x} \mid A \vec{x} = \vec{0} \} > 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
    3. The system \(A^T \vec{x} = \vec{b}\) has a unique solution for every \(\vec{b} \in \mathbb R^n\)
      🔗
    4. The columns of \(A\) are linearly independent.
      🔗
    Hint.
    Notice that \(A \) is square, so use the Invertible Matrix Theorem.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Option (b) is the only correct answer.
    🔗
    Option (a) is a true statement. Since \(A \) is square and \(A\vec{x} = \vec{b} \) has a unique solution for every \(\vec{b} \in \mathbb R^n \text{,}\) \(A \) is one-to-one (unique) and onto (always has a solution). Equivalently, \(A \) is invertible. So \(A \) has a pivot in every row, and thus the rows of \(A \) are linearly independent. Any \(n \) linearly independent vectors in \(\R^n \) form a basis for \(\R^n \text{,}\) so the rows of \(A \) form a basis for \(\R^n \text{.}\)
    🔗
    Option (b) is a false statement. Since \(A \) is invertible, the only solution to \(A \vec{x} = \vec{0} \) is \(\vec{x} = \vec{0} \text{,}\) so the dimension of the solution space to \(A \vec{x} = \vec{0} \) is 0.
    🔗
    Option (c) is a true statement. Since \(A \) is invertible, \(A^T \) is also invertible, and thus the system \(A^T \vec{x} = \vec{b} \) has a unique solution for every \(\vec{b} \in \mathbb R^n \text{.}\)
    🔗
    Option (d) is a true statement. Since \(A \) is invertible, the columns of \(A \) are linearly independent.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Problem 2.2.4.

    (2 points) Which of the following subsets of \(\mathbb R ^3\) are subspaces? \(\textit{Select all that apply}\)
    🔗
    1. \(\displaystyle S_1 = \{ (x,y,z) \in \mathbb R^3 : x-2y+z = 1 \}\)
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle S_2 = \{ s(1,0,1) + t(0,1,1) : s,t \in \mathbb R \}\)
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle S_3 = \{ (x,y,z) \in \mathbb R^3 : xy=0 \}\)
      🔗
    4. \(\displaystyle S_4 = \{ (x,y,z) \in \mathbb R^3 : x-2y+z = 0 \}\)
      🔗
    Hint.
    Every subspace can be written as the span of a collection of vectors, and vice versa. A set of vectors that is not a span will not be closed under addition and/or scalar multiplication.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Options (b) and (d) are correct.
    🔗
    Option (a) is incorrect because \(\vec{0} \notin S_1 \text{.}\)
    🔗
    Option (b) is correct because \(S_2 \) is the span of the vectors \((1,0,1) \) and \((0,1,1) \text{.}\)
    🔗
    Option (c) is incorrect because \(S_3 \) is not closed under addition. For example, \((1,0,0) \in S_3 \) and \((0,1,0) \in S_3 \text{,}\) but \((1,1,0) \notin S_3 \text{.}\)
    🔗
    Option (d) is correct because \(S_4 = \Nul(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}) \text{,}\) and the null space of a matrix is always a subspace.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Problem 2.2.5.

    (3 points) Suppose that \(\det \begin{pmatrix} a \amp b \\ c \amp d \end{pmatrix} = 2 \) and \(\det \begin{pmatrix} a \amp b \\ e \amp f \end{pmatrix} = 5\) and \(\det \begin{pmatrix} c \amp d \\ e \amp f \end{pmatrix} = -2\text{.}\) Find the value of the determinant below:
    \begin{equation*} \det \begin{pmatrix} a \amp 2 \amp b \\ c \amp 0 \amp d \\ e \amp 4 \amp f \end{pmatrix} = \framebox{$\phantom{$\begin{bmatrix} a \amp a \amp a \\ b \end{bmatrix}$}$ } \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Hint.
    Use cofactor expansion across the 2nd column.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Using cofactor expansion across the 2nd column, we get:
    \begin{align*} \det \begin{pmatrix} a \amp 2 \amp b \\ c \amp 0 \amp d \\ e \amp 4 \amp f \end{pmatrix} = -2 \det \begin{pmatrix} c \amp d \\ e \amp f \end{pmatrix} + 0 - 4 \det \begin{pmatrix} a \amp b \\ c \amp d \end{pmatrix}\\ = -2(-2) + 0 - 4(2) = -4 \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Problem 2.2.6.

    (3 points) Compute the area of the parallelogram \(\mathcal{S}\) having vertices \((-1,1), (0,4), (1,-4), \text{ and } (2,-1)\text{.}\) Then, let \(T : \mathbb R^2 \to \mathbb R^2\) be the transformation with standard matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}3 \amp 2 \\ 5 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\) Find the area of \(T(\mathcal{S})\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{Area}(\mathcal{S}) = \framebox{$\phantom{$\begin{bmatrix} a \amp a \amp a \\ b \end{bmatrix}$}$ } \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \text{Area}(T(\mathcal{S})) = \framebox{$\phantom{$\begin{bmatrix} a \amp a \amp a \\ b \end{bmatrix}$}$ } \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Pick an arbitrary vertex of the parallelogram, say \((-1,1)\text{.}\) Let us shift the whole parallogram such that this vertex is at the origin. The new vertices are then \((0,0), (1,3), (2,-5), \text{ and } (3,-2)\text{.}\) The area of the parallelogram is then given by the absolute value of the determinant of the matrix formed by the two adjacent edge vectors from the origin:
    \begin{equation*} \text{Area}(\mathcal{S}) = \left| \det \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp -5 \end{pmatrix} \right| = 11 \end{equation*}
    The transformation \(T\) has standard matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}3 \amp 2 \\ 5 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{,}\) so the area of the transformed parallelogram is:
    \begin{equation*} \text{Area}(T(\mathcal{S})) = \left| \det \begin{pmatrix}3 \amp 2 \\ 5 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix} \right| \cdot \text{Area}(\mathcal{S}) = 7 \cdot 11 = 77 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Problem 2.2.7.

    (4 points) Consider the system \(A \vec{x} = \vec{b}\text{,}\) where
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \amp 4 \\ 3 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix} \text{ and } \vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    Let \(A_1\) and \(A_2\) be as in Cramer’s Rule; that is, Let \(A_i\) be the matrix \(A\) with column \(i\) replaced with \(\vec{b}\text{.}\) Find \(\det(A), \det(A_1), \det(A_2)\text{,}\) and the solution vector \(\vec{x}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \det(A) = \framebox{$\phantom{$\begin{bmatrix} a \amp a \amp a \\ b \end{bmatrix}$}$ } \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \det(A_1) = \framebox{$\phantom{$\begin{bmatrix} a \amp a \amp a \\ b \end{bmatrix}$}$ } \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \det(A_2) = \framebox{$\phantom{$\begin{bmatrix} a \amp a \amp a \\ b \end{bmatrix}$}$ } \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x} = \framebox{$\phantom{$\begin{bmatrix} a \amp a \amp a \\ b \\ c \\ d \\ e \end{bmatrix}$}$ } \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Hint.
    Cramer’s Rule states that for a system \(A \vec{x} = \vec{b}\) with \(A\) invertible, the solution vector \(\vec{x}\) can be found using determinants:
    \begin{equation*} x_i = \frac{\det(A_i)}{\det(A)} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \det(A) = \begin{vmatrix} 7 \amp 4 \\ 3 \amp 2 \end{vmatrix} = 2 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \det(A_1) = \begin{vmatrix} 2 \amp 4 \\ 0 \amp 2 \end{vmatrix} = 4 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \det(A_2) = \begin{vmatrix} 7 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp 0 \end{vmatrix} = -6 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\det(A_1)}{\det(A)} \\ \frac{\det(A_2)}{\det(A)} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Problem 2.2.8.

    (5 points) Find an eigenvector \(\vec{v}\) of \(A\) corresponding to the eigenvalue \(\lambda = 3\)
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \amp 2 \\ -2 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{v} = \framebox{$\phantom{$\begin{bmatrix} a \amp a \amp a \\ b \\ c \\ d \\ e \end{bmatrix}$}$ } \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    An eigenvector \(\vec{v}\) corresponding to the eigenvalue \(\lambda = 3\) satisfies \((A - 3I)\vec{v} = \vec{0}\text{.}\) Solving this system gives:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (A - 3I) = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 2 \\ -2 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    and it is clear one nonzero vector in the null space of \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\) is \(\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Problem 2.2.9.

    (3 points) Find the value of \(h\) such that the matrix
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix}7 \amp h \\ 5 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    has eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 = -3\) and \(\lambda_2 = 9\)
    \begin{equation*} h = \framebox{$\phantom{$\begin{bmatrix} a \amp a \amp a \end{bmatrix}$}$ } \end{equation*}
    Hint.
    We want \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\) for the given eigenvalues.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    For \(A \) to have eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 = -3\) and \(\lambda_2 = 9\text{,}\) we must have:
    \begin{equation*} \det(A - \lambda_1 I) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \det(A - \lambda_2 I) = 0 \end{equation*}
    Solving these equations gives:
    \begin{equation*} \det(A - (-3)I) = \det\begin{bmatrix} 10 \amp h \\ 5 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix} = 20 - 5h = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad h = 4 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \det(A - 9I) = \det\begin{bmatrix} -2 \amp h \\ 5 \amp -10 \end{bmatrix} = 20 - 5h = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad h = 4 \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Problem 2.2.10.

    (6 points) \(\textbf{Show your work for part (b) on this page.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \end{equation*}
    Use the following Markov chain diagram to answer the questions.
    \begin{equation*} \end{equation*}
    (a) Find the stochastic matrix \(P\) of the Markov chain.
    \begin{equation*} \end{equation*}

    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \amp 3 \\ 2 \amp 5 \amp 2 \amp 4 \\ 3 \amp 4 \amp 3 \amp7 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ \amp \amp 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \amp 3 \\ 2 \amp 5 \amp 2 \amp 4 \\ 3 \amp 4 \amp 3 \amp7 \end{pmatrix} \end{equation*}
